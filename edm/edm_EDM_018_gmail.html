SECTION 02 — 市場洞察 / MARKET INSIGHT
Experiential Design

Not Louder, Better Designed: Where a Message Becomes a Feeling

People do not remember what they were told. They remember what they did. A programme can put its message on every screen in the city and still leave the room untouched, because information travels easily and experience does not. The work that changes behaviour is narrower and harder: building a moment a person chooses to step into — something to touch, solve, carry away or stand inside — so that the point behind the policy arrives as a feeling rather than a sentence. That is the difference between announcing a message and staging it, and unlike volume it can be measured. The three numbers below are what that difference looks like once someone finally counts it.

Longer attention at a hands-on exhibit, Sandifer 2003

The most basic measure of engagement is time. Tracking visitors at a science centre, Sandifer found the average time spent per exhibit ran close to three times the 30-second average recorded at museums with non-interactive dioramas. Nothing about the information changed — the same content sat on the wall. What changed is that a visitor had something to do with their hands, and a reason to stay in front of it. Three times the attention is three times the chance the message is still with them when they walk out.

More beliefs attached by emotional campaigns, IPA 2013

Across the IPA Databank, campaigns built on emotional engagement produced an average of 1.7 brand effects — associated beliefs about the organisation — against 1.0 for purely rational campaigns. That is a measure of how much of a message actually attaches to what people think afterwards. Binet and Field’s conclusion is uncomfortable for anyone who assumes a clearer explanation makes a better campaign: being understood is not the same as being believed. Feeling something is what makes a message stay.

Live audience recall against watching alone, iScience 2025

A 2025 study in iScience tracked audiences watching the identical dance performance live, in a cinema, and alone on a screen. More than half of the live audience named the moment the performers looked at them — 51.5%, against 17.9% of those watching alone. But the most useful finding is in the detail: watching together in a cinema reached 43.5%, statistically indistinguishable from live. So it is not simply ‘in person’. It is the room, the shared attention and the sense that the moment was meant for you — a designed condition, not a delivery channel. Which is why we start from the person rather than the poster. Before a panel is drawn we ask what someone should do, feel or remember in the moment they meet the work, and then build backwards from that moment: what they hold, where the sign stands, what makes them want to try. 唔係大聲啲，係設計得好啲 — the message was never the problem. The moment was.
 
SECTION 03 — / CASE STUDY
GOVERNMENT政制及內地事務局 Constitutional and Mainland Affairs Bureau (CMAB)

Turning the Basic Law into a bus stop nobody walks past

A legal statute, a shopping mall, and an owl: how civic education learned to hold attention.

Constitutional and legal education usually arrives as text-heavy panels and leaflets — a format Hong Kong audiences walk straight past. For CMAB’s 2026 roving workshop series, Firebean rebuilt the format around one piece of kinetic retail architecture: a large bus-stop installation that reads as a photo landmark from outside, while the reception desk and material storage hide inside it. The Basic Law’s core points were rewritten as stages of an owl-led bus journey rather than clauses on a board, so an abstract statute became a physical place families queue to enter. The series toured malls including tmtplaza, SPOT and MOSTown, carrying one identity across every stop — letting recognition accumulate district by district instead of restarting from zero at each venue.
