SECTION 02 — 市場洞察 / MARKET INSIGHT
Public Engagement by Design

Not Louder, Better Designed: The Last Metre of Public Engagement

Public engagement rarely fails for lack of information. It fails in the last metre. Policy is written in documents; behaviour happens on site — and the space between the two is where the work actually is. When a programme underdelivers, the instinct is to turn up the volume: more reminders, more channels, more urgency. It rarely works, because the audience was never short of a message. They were short of a moment — the second at which taking part becomes the easiest thing available. That is a design problem rather than a communications one, and — unlike volume — it can be measured. The numbers below describe the market that surrounds that moment: what Hong Kong already spends, how much activity it already runs, and what the physical layer of public engagement is worth.

Information Services Department budget, 2026-27

The Information Services Department’s approved expenditure for 2026-27 is HK$651,127,000 under Head 74. The official notes are explicit that publicity spending sits mainly with individual bureaux and departments, and that messages travel through television, radio, digital media, print, outdoor advertising and public engagement activities. Public engagement is not an add-on to the budget — it is a standing line inside it, carrying annual accountability with it.

Public events assisted in 2025

In 2025 the department assisted 680 public events, against 571 in 2024; the 2026-27 estimate is 570. The official footnote explains the 2025 peak: intensified publicity for the Legislative Council election and the National Games. For anyone planning one of those events, the number sets the standard — 680 occasions in a single year on which a message had to work in a real room, with a real crowd, a real queue and a real sign.

Largest exhibition design and build award

Award notices published by the Planning Department show the scale of individual exhibition commissions. Design, production and maintenance of the City Gallery’s second-floor exhibition went for HK$19,785,000; the first-floor exhibition for HK$14,355,000; the ground-floor exhibition for HK$7,960,000. The scope is written into the contract title itself: design, production and maintenance. That is the market that builds the room. What the room then makes a visitor do is a separate design decision — and the one most often left unwritten. Which brings it back to the last metre. Every one of those events and every one of those builds ends in the same place: a person standing in front of something, deciding whether to stop, read, ask, sign, queue or walk on. Louder does not reach that moment. Better designed does. 唔係大聲啲，係設計得好啲 — and that is the part of public engagement we take responsibility for.
 
SECTION 03 — / CASE STUDY
GOVERNMENT政制及內地事務局 Constitutional and Mainland Affairs Bureau (CMAB)

Turning the Basic Law into a bus stop nobody walks past

A legal statute, a shopping mall, and an owl: how civic education learned to hold attention.

Constitutional and legal education usually arrives as text-heavy panels and leaflets — a format Hong Kong audiences walk straight past. For CMAB’s 2026 roving workshop series, Firebean rebuilt the format around one piece of kinetic retail architecture: a large bus-stop installation that reads as a photo landmark from outside, while the reception desk and material storage hide inside it. The Basic Law’s core points were rewritten as stages of an owl-led bus journey rather than clauses on a board, so an abstract statute became a physical place families queue to enter. The series toured malls including tmtplaza, SPOT and MOSTown, carrying one identity across every stop — letting recognition accumulate district by district instead of restarting from zero at each venue.
