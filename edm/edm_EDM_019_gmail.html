SECTION 02 — 市場洞察 / MARKET INSIGHT
Gamified Learning

Play as Policy: Why Gamified Civic Education Works

A statute is an abstraction, and abstractions do not survive a shopping mall. That is the design problem facing every civic education campaign: the content is fixed, serious and often dry, and the audience is a family with a pram and ninety seconds to spare. The instinct is to explain harder — bigger panels, more text, clearer diagrams. The evidence points somewhere else. When people are given something to operate — a flight stick, a maze, a claw, a role — they learn more, and they keep more of it, than when they are simply told. What follows is not an argument for making public messages trivial. It is an argument for making them operable.

Hands-on simulation beats listening, Chernikova et al. 2020

A meta-analysis of 145 studies on simulation-based and role-play training found a large overall learning effect of 0.85 standard deviations — among the strongest results in the literature on how people acquire complex skills. The mechanism is not novelty. A simulation forces a decision, and a decision requires the learner to hold the rule in mind while using it. A cockpit, a maze and a claw machine all do the same job: they convert a clause into an operation.

Lecture students more likely to fail, Freeman et al. PNAS 2014

Freeman and colleagues pooled 225 studies comparing lecturing with active learning in science teaching. Students in traditional lecture classes were roughly 1.5 times more likely to fail, and active-learning classes lifted exam performance by about 6%. The size of that gap is what makes it useful for public communication: it is not a marginal improvement. It is the difference between a message that was delivered and a message that was used.

Retention gain without a fun bonus, Wouters et al. 2013

Wouters and colleagues analysed 77 studies and found serious games beat conventional instruction on learning (0.29 SD) and, more importantly, on delayed retention (0.36 SD). The finding that matters most sits in the negative result: the games were not significantly more motivating. Play is not the point. Retention is. A claw machine that leaves a child holding a souvenir but no rule in mind has entertained someone — it has not taught them. The design test is whether the play is carrying the content, or replacing it. That is the line we work on. A game is not a reward bolted onto a policy message; it is the form the message takes so that it can be operated. 好玩唔係目的，留住先係 — the measure of a good game is not the queue at the booth. It is what a family can still explain on the way home.
 
SECTION 03 — / CASE STUDY
GOVERNMENT政制及內地事務局 Constitutional and Mainland Affairs Bureau (CMAB)

The Basic Law as a mission briefing: turning statutes into a cockpit

An owl, a drone and a mech suit — civic education people queue to play.

For a campaign where the subject is a legal statute, the client's problem was attention rather than accuracy: the content had to be right, but nobody was going to queue for a text panel. Firebean turned the roving exhibition into a robotics academy. A drone obstacle course, a remote-control mech maze, a simulated cockpit mission led by an owl mascot and a claw machine for souvenirs — each station asks the visitor to operate something, and the constitutional points sit inside the mission rather than on a board beside it. The series toured malls including Citywalk2, Olympian City 2 and The Wai, carrying one mechanical language across every stop.
