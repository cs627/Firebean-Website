Design Iteration

Better, Not Perfect: Why a Public Message Needs a Prototype, Not a Poster

A policy paper can be perfect and still fail in a lobby. The wording is argued over in committee, signed off, translated, printed — and then the first visitor walks in, stops four metres from the panel, reads nothing, and leaves. None of that is a copywriting problem. It is the height of the sign, the direction of the queue, the first question on the form, the object nobody wants to pick up. Design has known this for a century: version one is not a draft to be hidden, it is the instrument that tells you what the real thing is. deTour's theme this year is 'BETTER, NOT PERFECT: Prototypes for Tomorrow' — the prototype is honest in a way no finished deck can be. Public communication is close to the last discipline still sending finished work to its first rehearsal. What follows is not an argument for lowering the standard. It is an argument for testing in the room before you print.

One round of five users finds most of it, Faulkner 2003

Faulkner replayed the famous five-user claim as an experiment: 100 separate rounds of five users, drawn from a pool of 60, on one benchmark task. A single round of five found 85% of the problem areas on average — but the worst round found 55%. The point is not that testing is unnecessary. It is that one round is a sample, and a sample is a range: you test, you fix, you test again, because the floor is 55%, not 85%. A pilot is a measurement, not a verdict. (Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers 35(3): 379-383)

Approved is not the same as understood, Noble et al. 2022

Regulators expect at least 80% of patients to answer comprehension questions about a patient information leaflet correctly. Noble and colleagues asked the actual patients — 35 people with epilepsy — and got 54% for one medicine and 62% for another. They then asked a convenient proxy group, 262 university students, and got 86% and 90%. Same document, same questions, roughly a thirty-point gap created entirely by who was in the room. Sign-off had certified the paper; it had not tested the reader. (Epilepsia 63(8): 2130-2143)

Three rounds, not one, before it passed, Smith et al. 2013

A colorectal screening leaflet was developed against a pass rule fixed in advance: at least 80% correct answers on all eight comprehension statements. Round one failed two statements. After revision, round two failed one. Only in round three did every statement clear and the leaflet was deemed fit for purpose. Nobody changed the topic between rounds; they changed the sentences, the order and the examples. That is what prototyping looks like in public communication — not a better writer, a faster loop. (Patient Education and Counseling 93(3): 619-625)

Version Two Was the Design: a roadshow that learned between buildings

Same safety content, a different lobby each time — and a design allowed to answer back.

The Civil Service Bureau had a subject that resists posters: occupational safety and health is technical, procedural, and easy to walk past. Its 2026 roadshow toured government buildings — the Central Government Offices, the Trade and Industry Tower and others — where the audience is staff passing on the way to a meeting, not visitors who came for an exhibition. Firebean rebuilt the language of the show rather than the wording of the rules: a stainless-steel and iron-bracket structure matched to the technology on display, spinning display boards that turn dense guidance into something a passer-by can operate with one hand, and a mobile quiz that asks people to answer rather than read. Because the exhibition is modular and built to travel, each building taught us something the previous one could not — the quiet advantage of a roving format: you get to ship version two, and then version three.
